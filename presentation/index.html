Your name · Roll number · Department · Guide
Good morning. This is Pramana, a legal research assistant for Indian criminal law. The name is Sanskrit for proof, or a valid means of knowing, and that is the design goal: nothing reaches the lawyer unless it can be proved from data the system actually holds. A lawyer gives it the facts of a case and the date of the offence. It works out which criminal code applies, finds the sections the facts disclose, checks each one against a graph built from the statutes, finds precedents, drafts counter-arguments, and shows how appeals on those sections were decided. All of it runs on a laptop with a 4 GB GPU, at zero cost.
The problem · renumbering

On 1 July 2024 India renumbered its criminal law.

No such section. The BNS ends at 358.

A section number means nothing without its code, and the offence date decides the code.

The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita replaced the Indian Penal Code on 1 July 2024, and almost every offence got a new number. Offences committed before that date are still tried under the IPC, so for years courts will apply both codes. [click] Section 302 is murder in the IPC. In the BNS it is hurting religious feelings. [click] 303 was murder by a life-convict; in the BNS it is theft. [click] And 420, the most famous cheating section, does not exist in the BNS at all. So a citation that is correct in one code is a different crime, or nothing, in the other. [click] The offence date decides which code applies, and everything downstream depends on getting that right.

Stage 0 · the offence-date router

The offence date picks the code. No model is involved.

# src/pipeline/router.py
def route(offence_date):
    d = parse_date(offence_date)  # strict ISO date
    return "ipc" if d < transition_date() else "bns"
  • Runs before anything else. Every later lookup uses only this code.
  • An ambiguous date is never guessed. A guessed date is a guessed code.
  • A course of conduct that crosses the date is analysed under both codes.
Stage 0 is deliberately boring: one comparison, no model. [click] An offence on 30 June 2024 goes to the IPC; [click] one on 1 July goes to the BNS. Both boundary days are in our tests. [click] Our motorcycle case, 15 August 2024, is BNS. From here on, retrieval searches only that code’s sections, Node 2 verifies only against that code, and only that era’s judgments vote. Dates are parsed strictly: 08/09/2024 is 8 September in India and 9 August in America, and a guessed date is a guessed code. An offence spread over a period that crosses the date, such as cruelty from January to September 2024, is analysed under both codes.

Why the domain model lost: hubness

For a motorcycle theft, InLegal-Sbert’s top three were:

  1. IPC 443 Lurking house-trespass
  2. IPC 278 Making atmosphere noxious to health
  3. IPC 86 Offence by one who is intoxicated

Theft, IPC 378 / 379, first appeared at rank 311. The same few sections topped almost every query.

Retrieval needs an embedding model. The obvious choice was InLegal-Sbert, trained on 5.4 million Indian legal documents. We didn’t assume; we ran a bake-off on fifteen hand-written cases. The general multilingual model BGE-M3 got recall at 3 of 0.53 against 0.20, and led on every other measure. [click] The reason is hubness: the domain model puts the same handful of sections at the top for almost every query. For a motorcycle theft its top three were lurking house-trespass, noxious atmosphere and intoxication, and theft first appeared at rank 311. A 2025 paper on Indian prior-case retrieval found the same thing, and we reproduced it on our own data.
Retrieval · two signals, fused

Statute text finds what a section says. Precedents show what courts charged.

Top 6 of 14 shown to Node 1. Real output of the saved sample.
Retrieval uses two signals. The first is plain similarity between the facts and statute text; long sections are indexed by sub-section so one clause isn’t drowned by the rest. The second comes from judgments: we find judgments similar to the facts, and each votes for the sections it applied. Statute text tends to find the definition, theft; precedent votes find what courts actually cite. The two rankings are combined by reciprocal rank fusion with the textbook k of 60, which beat everything else we tried. A safety detail: an IPC judgment can only vote in a BNS case through the verified IPC-to-BNS mapping. [click] For the motorcycle case, theft, BNS 303, comes first. These candidates are what the model reads.

This is the heart of the system. We build a graph with NetworkX from the data we actually ingested: every section of both codes as a node, every judgment as a node, an edge whenever a judgment cites a section, and a reviewed mapping edge from each IPC section to its BNS counterpart. The verification rule is one line: a section is real only if it is a node of the code the router chose. On the right is a real piece of the graph around theft. Judgments cite IPC 378, 379 and 411. The BNS sections on the right have no judgments of their own yet; they connect only through the mapping. [click] The graph had to pass a binary gate: ten real and ten invented section numbers, including traps such as BNS 420 and 498A, which exist only in the IPC. It scored 20 out of 20.

When Node 2 rejects a proposal, the case goes back to Node 1 with the specific reason, at most twice. This is the real adversarial gate run: a BNS theft case with retrieval deliberately withheld. [click] Round 0: the model says 421. Rejected: not a section of the BNS. [click] Round 1: it says 378, the IPC theft section. One lesson from building it: when we sent the reason as a bare follow-up message, the model repeated 421 three times. Replaying its own answer and listing the banned numbers fixed it. Node 2b · applicability

A real section can still be the wrong charge.

The judge, a language model

It reads each verified section’s own text against the facts, twice. A section is marked supported only if both passes agree. Otherwise it is shown as doubted, with the missing ingredient named. Nothing is hidden.

Verification proves a section exists, never that it applies. Early on, a cheating case with context withheld got BNS 66, 67 and 68, which are sexual offences; all three exist, so all three verified. So Node 2b, an applicability judge, reads each verified section’s own text against the facts, twice. A section shows as supported only if both passes agree; otherwise it is shown as doubted, with the missing ingredient named, never silently dropped. [click] Around the judge sit deterministic legal rules: a lesser offence shown as included in the aggravated one, definitions charged under their punishing section, the injury grade choosing the hurt section, common intention when several accused act together, and so on. Each rule has a switch so we could measure it. [click] On the development set, the share of supported charges that were right went from 44 to 58 percent, and over-charging fell from 3.5 to 2.6 sections a case.

Precedents come only from graph edges, so every precedent really did apply the charged section. That creates a problem for new cases. [click] In our corpus, not a single judgment applies BNS 303; BNS theft cases have not reached the higher courts in bulk yet. [click] So Node 3 crosses the reviewed mapping to the IPC predecessors: 378 with 11 judgments and 379 with 158. The law of theft did not change, so that precedent is still relevant. All 575 rows of the mapping were reviewed against the gazette text; 539 are verified bridges. [click] The candidates are ranked by similarity to the facts. Near-identical companion appeals are removed, so you don’t see one judgment three times. The judgment pool is fetched while the embedder still holds the GPU, before the language model loads.

Everything runs on an RTX 3050 Ti laptop GPU with 4 GB of memory. The embedder and the language model cannot both fit. So each run choreographs the card. [click] First we tell Ollama to unload the language model and wait until it is really gone. [click] Then BGE-M3 loads in half precision, encodes the facts and prefetches the pool of candidate precedents. [click] Then it is freed and the language model loads for the three steps that need it. [click] Moving the query encode from CPU onto the freed GPU halved the time before generation, from 36 to 18 seconds. There is no recurring cost, no API key, and the whole project has 413 automated tests.

Now the honest part. IL-TUR is the published Indian legal benchmark from ACL 2024. On statute identification, the best supervised model, trained on 42 thousand documents, scores 28.1 macro-F1. Pramana is zero-shot, a 4-billion-parameter model on a laptop, and scores 11.8 on the test split, which we ran exactly once after all decisions were made on dev, where it scored 9.8. The two splits' confidence intervals overlap, so read it as the same performance confirmed, not an improvement. We are far below the supervised state of the art and we say so. [click] It does well on grave, common offences: murder at 0.78 F1. [click] It is weak on minor companion offences such as simple hurt or insult, mostly because those sections never reach the candidate list: only 37 percent of the right sections did. That points to the next fix: retrieval coverage, not a bigger model.

Limitations
  • Over-charging: real, plausible extra sections (34, 307, 149)
  • Minor companion offences rarely retrieved
  • The IPC-BNS mapping was reviewed against gazette text, not yet by a practising lawyer
  • Counter-argument quality has no metric yet
  • Prior-case retrieval is not benchmarked
Next
  • Retrieval coverage for companion offences
  • A lawyer’s review of the mapping and the rules

We also kept a record of what didn’t work, because every one of these was a reasonable idea. The legal-domain embedder lost the bake-off. A cross-encoder reranker made retrieval worse: it rewards word overlap, and facts and statute text use different words. Asking the model twice with two orderings found nothing new and cut precision. A companion-offence rule failed the merge rules we set before running it, so it was not merged. We never fine-tuned, by design: published results show a fine-tuned Indian legal model scoring below its own base model. [click] Limitations: over-charging, missed minor offences, the mapping not yet reviewed by a practising lawyer, and no metric yet for argument quality.
